Skeleton

A person made of dots and sticks.

Each dot is a body landmark, such as a wrist or an ankle. The lines connect the dots. The program has 17 possible dots, but the jumping-jack referee only needs six.

Two colors tell you whose side.

Cyan means the person’s LEFT.
Orange means the person’s RIGHT.

Use the person’s own left and right, as if you were wearing their shoes. In this front-facing picture, their left appears on your right.

The little numbers, such as 5, 6, 9, 10, 15, 16, are dot IDs. They are not angles, scores or repetition counts.

The dotted lines are little fences.

Upper blue dashed lines: each wrist must go above its own shoulder’s fence for OPEN.

Lower green dashed lines: each wrist must go below its own shoulder’s fence for CLOSED.

“y grows downward” means lower dots have bigger y numbers. Moving your hand up makes its y number smaller.

The bars below the feet are rulers.

The short green bar shows the largest allowed CLOSED foot spread: 1.0 shoulder widths.

The longer blue bar shows the smallest allowed OPEN foot spread: 1.4 shoulder widths.

Both are centered between the current ankles. They show required widths, not fixed places where each foot must land. The small yellow line across the shoulders shows the shoulder-width ruler.

What if a dot is hard to see?

A known but low-confidence dot is drawn as a red outline; connected sticks become dim. A dot with missing coordinates cannot be drawn.

If the whole chosen person is missing, the panel says SELECTED PERSON MISSING. The debugger does not invent a replacement pose.

The outer rectangle is the original camera picture’s boundary. There is no video background in this view.

T shows or hides the guide rulers. I shows or hides the extra dot IDs. The six required IDs remain visible.

Body-relative measurements

Use the person as the ruler.

A nearby person looks big. A faraway person looks small. Dividing by the person’s own size helps the referee use the same rules for both.

Wrist dY / span

“How far below or above my own shoulder is my wrist?”

First measure the wrist’s vertical distance from its same-side shoulder. Then divide by the vertical distance from the average shoulder level to the average ankle level.

In your pictureMeaning
L 0.432Left wrist is below its shoulder by about 43.2% of that body span.
R 0.415Right wrist is below its shoulder by about 41.5% of that span.

Both are bigger than +0.1, so both wrists pass the CLOSED rule. To pass OPEN, both must be smaller than −0.1.

Feet / shoulder width: 0.642

“How wide apart are the ankles, compared with the shoulders?”

The feet are about 0.642 shoulder widths apart: a little under two-thirds of one shoulder width.

C ≤ 1.0: close enough for CLOSED.
O ≥ 1.4: wide enough for OPEN.

0.642 ≤ 1.0 passes CLOSED.
0.642 ≥ 1.4 is false, so OPEN fails.

Only the horizontal distance between the ankles is used. The referee does not use their diagonal distance.

Read the two little gauges like number lines. The L and R dots show the wrist values; F shows the feet value. Colored ticks mark the rule boundaries. The wrist gauge runs from negative “up” values to positive “down” values. The gray track is not a progress bar toward earning a jump.
The exact arithmetic, if you want to peek

The screen rounds most numbers to three decimal places. These calculations use the saved, unrounded coordinates, so calculating from the displayed table can differ a little.

shoulder width = |left shoulder x − right shoulder x| ≈ 0.0828125
body span = average ankle y − average shoulder y ≈ 0.521875
wrist margin = 0.100 × body span ≈ 0.0521875
left wrist dY ≈ 0.225347; left dY / span ≈ 0.432
right wrist dY ≈ 0.216319; right dY / span ≈ 0.415
ankle horizontal spread ≈ 0.053125
feet ratio = 0.053125 / 0.0828125 ≈ 0.642

Each shoulder has its own wrist fences: shoulder y minus the margin for OPEN, and shoulder y plus the margin for CLOSED. These fences move with the current pose. Gauge dots are visually clipped at the ends of the number line; their printed values still show the measurement.

Confidence = “How sure is the detector?”

The score is between 0 and 1. A bigger score means the detector is more confident about that dot. Every required dot must score at least 0.500.

The green mini-bar shows the score. Its little white tick marks the cutoff, 0.500.

A score of 0.995 is high confidence. It is not a jump score or a promise of exactly 99.5% accuracy.

x and y = “Where is the dot?”

Imagine the picture as a sheet of graph paper. x goes from left to right. y goes from top to bottom.

x = 0.550 means 55% of the way across the picture. y = 0.353 means 35.3% of the way down. These are fractions of the picture, not meters.

PASS means the dot has usable numbers and enough confidence. It does not mean an entire jumping jack has passed.

One bad required dot is enough to make the referee forget the current phase. LOW CONF means the dot is not trusted. NaN xy means its position is missing. Lowering confidence cannot bring back a position that was already saved as missing.
What about the other 11 dots?

They help draw the person, but they are not jumping-jack gates. Elbows, knees and the nose do not decide this count. Selection of a person before a workout uses other rules; those are separate.

All three saved values for a required joint (x, y and confidence) must be finite. “Finite” means an ordinary usable number, not NaN or infinity. This debugger’s classifier does not add a separate rule requiring every coordinate to be inside 0–1; a finite off-camera point is not automatically rejected just for being outside the picture.

For this 1280 × 720 source, the rounded shoulder coordinate (0.550, 0.353) is roughly pixel (704, 254). The exact unrounded values are used for the actual rules.

How to try a change in the Python window: click a row to select it, then press , to decrease or . to increase. Or click that row’s little − / + boxes. The highlighted first row in your screenshot is the selected confidence knob. B restores all seven current source defaults.

An edit pauses playback and recalculates the whole replay. Edits stay in memory: the recording and Python source are unchanged. On the keyboard, −/+ change playback speed; ,/. change the selected threshold.

What do the comparison signs mean?

≥ means “at least.” ≤ means “at most.” > means “strictly bigger.” < means “strictly smaller.”

Exactly matching the confidence, feet, minimum-shoulder-width, confirmation-time or maximum-gap boundary is allowed. Wrist boundaries are strict: a wrist exactly on its fence does not pass. Body span must be strictly greater than zero.

The controls have bounds: confidence 0–1; both feet ratios 0–10; wrist margin 0–2; confirmation 0–5 seconds; sample gap 0–10 seconds; minimum shoulder width 0.000001–1. A zero confirmation time confirms a matching pose immediately.

Raw posture: CLOSED means “this one picture looks closed.” All three CLOSED checks pass. The OPEN column fails because the person is not making the open shape. That is normal at the start.

DEAD BAND = “In between.”

The dots are usable, but neither full pose passes. Maybe the hands are moving past shoulder height, or the feet are between the close and wide limits.

Keep the remembered stable pose, but cancel the hold timer. A new uninterrupted matching run must start its timer again.

INVALID = “I cannot judge this.”

The selected person is missing, a required dot is unusable, the shoulders are too narrow, or the ankle level is not below the shoulder level.

Forget the phase: stable becomes UNKNOWN and the pending timer is cleared. The existing repetition total is retained by this offline replay. Gate cells show N/A when the geometry cannot be judged.

The gap between the CLOSED and OPEN rules stops tiny wobbles from rapidly switching the remembered pose. Both wrists are tested against their own shoulders.

What makes the referee forget, and what stays?

A missing person, an unusable required dot, a too-small shoulder width, a nonpositive body span, a track/generation change, or too large a time gap resets the phase to UNKNOWN. The counter then needs a confirmed CLOSED pose to start a new cycle.

DEAD BAND is gentler: it cancels pending confirmation but keeps the stable phase. One dead-band sample can interrupt a hold even when the next sample returns to the same raw pose.

This all-sample replay carries its accumulated repetition total across generation changes. Forgetting the current phase is not the same as erasing already counted repetitions. Reaching the confirmation time still requires a saved sample; the debugger does not create an observation between samples.

Extra things behind H help

The normal workout’s person-selection gesture is separate from the jumping-jack checks: confidence at least 0.5, the person’s RIGHT wrist above the nose, and RIGHT elbow above RIGHT shoulder. The current start hold is 2 seconds; reacquisition hold is 1 second; missing grace is 0.15 seconds. The normal session target is 30 seconds.

Those are not extra jumping-jack gates, and they do not turn this imported 5.17-second clip into a 30-second recording. Pre-start observations and other candidate people are not saved here, so their selection holds cannot be reconstructed.

The console summary can report how many samples fail particular checks. Several checks can fail in the same sample. Those overlapping numbers are not a count of missed jumping jacks.

An easy reading order

Next time the screen feels busy…

  1. Check the six dots. Is each required joint usable?
  2. Check the pose column. Do all three CLOSED or OPEN checks pass?
  3. Check the timer. Has that same pose lasted long enough?
  4. Check the memory. Has CLOSED → OPEN → CLOSED finished? That is when the count goes up.
